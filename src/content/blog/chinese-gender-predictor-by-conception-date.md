Many people find that process confusing or error-prone — particularly for dates that fall near Chinese New Year in January and February, where a single day can shift both the lunar year and the lunar age.

## How the Conception-Date Method Works

Understanding the underlying process helps you appreciate what the tool is doing and why each input matters.

### Step 1: Enter the Mother's Date of Birth

The mother's date of birth is the starting point for calculating her traditional Chinese lunar age at the time of conception. The tool converts this date into its equivalent position on the Chinese lunisolar calendar — specifically, it identifies which lunar year the mother was born in, accounting for whether her birth date fell before or after Chinese New Year in that year.

### Step 2: Enter the Estimated Conception Date

The estimated conception date tells the tool *when* conception likely occurred. This is used for two purposes: to identify the lunar year of conception (needed to complete the age calculation) and to identify the lunar *month* of conception (the second coordinate on the Qing Gong Biao chart).

The conception date is an estimate in almost every case. That is expected and acceptable — the tool works with your best available approximation.

### Step 3: Convert the Dates to the Traditional Lunar Calendar

The Chinese lunisolar calendar does not align neatly with the Gregorian calendar. A Chinese lunar month begins on the day of a new moon and lasts approximately 29–30 days. Chinese New Year — the first day of the lunar year — falls on a different Gregorian date each year, typically between January 21 and February 20.

Because of this, a date in March on the Gregorian calendar might correspond to Lunar Month 2 or Lunar Month 3, depending on when that year's new moons fell. The tool references historical astronomical data to perform this conversion precisely for both the birth date and the conception date.

### Step 4: Determine the Traditional Lunar Age

Once both dates have been mapped to their positions in the Chinese lunisolar calendar, the tool calculates the mother's traditional Chinese lunar age at the time of conception.

Traditional Chinese age reckoning (known as *xusui*, 虚岁) differs from Western chronological age in two key ways: a newborn is counted as age 1 from birth, and everyone's age advances on Chinese New Year rather than on individual birthdays. This means the traditional lunar age is typically 1 to 2 years higher than a person's Western age — but the exact amount depends on the relationship between the person's birth date, the conception date, and the lunar year boundaries for both years.

Applying a blanket rule such as "always add 1" can produce the wrong result, particularly for births or conceptions that fall in January or February near Chinese New Year. The tool handles this correctly by working from the actual lunisolar calendar rather than a simplified approximation.

For a detailed explanation of how this age system works, see [Chinese Lunar Age Explained](https://freegenderpredictor.com/blog/chinese-lunar-age-explained) and [How to Calculate Your Chinese Lunar Age for the Gender Calendar](https://freegenderpredictor.com/blog/how-to-calculate-chinese-lunar-age).

### Step 5: Determine the Lunar Conception Month

In parallel with the age calculation, the tool maps the estimated conception date to the corresponding month in the Chinese lunisolar calendar. This produces a number between 1 and 12 (or occasionally a leap-month designation in years when the lunar calendar adds a 13th month).

The lunar conception month can differ from the Gregorian conception month. For example, a conception date in late January 2025 — before that year's Chinese New Year on January 29 — would fall in Lunar Month 12 of the previous lunar year rather than Lunar Month 1 of 2025. The tool accounts for this automatically.

### Step 6: Look Up the Qing Gong Biao Chart

With the traditional lunar age and the lunar conception month in hand, the tool looks up the corresponding cell on the [Qing Gong Biao](https://freegenderpredictor.com/blog/qing-gong-biao-history) — the authentic imperial Chinese gender prediction chart. The chart is structured as a grid:

- **Vertical axis (rows):** The mother's traditional Chinese lunar age, typically covering ages 18 to 45.
- **Horizontal axis (columns):** The Chinese lunar month of conception, covering Months 1 through 12.

The cell where the two values intersect displays the traditional prediction: **Boy** or **Girl**.

## Why Does the Conception Date Matter?

The traditional Qing Gong Biao was designed around the *month in which fertilization occurred*, not the month in which a baby was expected to arrive. This is the central reason why conception date — not due date — is the relevant input.

Consider the difference between these three related concepts:

| Date Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Conception date** | The estimated day fertilization occurred, roughly at ovulation |
| **Due date** | The estimated delivery date, approximately 38–40 weeks after conception |
| **Lunar conception month** | The Chinese lunar month that contains the estimated conception date |

A typical pregnancy lasts around 266 days from conception to delivery. A due date therefore falls in an entirely different Gregorian month than the conception date — and almost certainly in a different Chinese lunar month as well. Entering a due date as though it were a conception date would produce a chart lookup in a completely unrelated column, generating a meaningless result.

Conception timing is also often estimated rather than known with precision. This is normal. See [Conception Date vs. Due Date: Which Should You Use for Gender Prediction?](https://freegenderpredictor.com/blog/conception-date-vs-due-date-gender-prediction) for detailed guidance on how to estimate your conception date from an ultrasound, ovulation tracking data, or your last menstrual period.

## Conception Date vs. Due Date: Which One Should You Enter?

This is the most common source of confusion when using any Chinese gender chart calculator.

- **Conception date** is approximately when the egg was fertilized by a sperm cell, typically around ovulation.
- **Due date** (EDD) is an estimate of when the baby is expected to be born, usually 40 weeks from the first day of the last menstrual period, or 38 weeks from conception.

These two dates are not interchangeable. They differ by roughly 266 days and will almost always fall in different Chinese lunar months. Entering a due date into a field designed for a conception date will produce an incorrect traditional chart lookup.

Always enter the **estimated conception date**, never the due date.

## How Is Traditional Chinese Lunar Age Different From Your Actual Age?

Your everyday Western age is a measure of completed years since your date of birth, based on the Gregorian solar calendar.

Traditional Chinese lunar age (*xusui*, 虚岁) follows a different convention:

- A newborn is considered **1 year old at birth**, reflecting the cultural recognition of the gestation period as life already underway.
- Everyone's age advances by one year on **Chinese New Year** (the Spring Festival), not on individual birthdays.

As a result, the traditional lunar age is typically 1 to 2 years higher than the Western chronological age. The exact difference depends on whether the mother was born before or after Chinese New Year in her birth year, and whether Chinese New Year has already occurred in the year of conception.

This matters for the Qing Gong Biao chart because the chart's rows were calibrated for traditional lunar ages. Using a Western age shifts the lookup coordinate by one or two rows, and adjacent rows on the chart often show opposite predictions. The tool calculates the correct traditional value for you automatically.

## How Is the Lunar Conception Month Determined?

The tool converts the estimated Gregorian conception date into the corresponding Chinese lunar month by referencing the historical record of new-moon dates for that year.

A few important points about this conversion:

- **Lunar months do not align with Gregorian months.** A lunar month begins on the day of a new moon and lasts approximately 29–30 days, meaning a single lunar month can span parts of two consecutive Gregorian months.
- **Chinese New Year is the boundary between lunar years.** If the conception date falls in January or early February before that year's Chinese New Year, it belongs to the final lunar month of the *previous* lunar year, not the first month of the new one.
- **Leap lunar months occur roughly every two to three years.** When the lunar calendar inserts an intercalary (leap) month to stay aligned with the solar year, conception dates that fall within that leap month require specific traditional handling. The tool manages this internally.

Because these conversions depend on the actual astronomical lunar calendar — which shifts slightly every year — they cannot be done reliably with a simple rule of thumb. This is precisely why an automated calculator that references historical ephemeris data is the most dependable approach.

## Example: Using a Conception Date

Here is one illustrative example showing how the full process works end to end.

**Scenario:**
A mother was born on **August 14, 1996**. Her estimated conception date is **October 10, 2025**.

**Step 1 — Determine the lunar birth year:**
Chinese New Year in 1996 fell on February 19. Because August 14 is after February 19, her **lunar birth year is 1996**.

**Step 2 — Determine the lunar conception year:**
Chinese New Year in 2025 fell on January 29. Because October 10 is after January 29, her **lunar conception year is 2025**.

**Step 3 — Calculate the traditional lunar age:**
$$\text{Lunar Age} = (\text{Lunar Conception Year} - \text{Lunar Birth Year}) + 1 = (2025 - 1996) + 1 = \mathbf{30}$$

Her Western age at conception was 29. Her **traditional lunar age for the chart is 30**.

**Step 4 — Determine the lunar conception month:**
October 10, 2025 falls within **Chinese Lunar Month 8** of that lunar year (the eighth new-moon cycle after Chinese New Year on January 29, 2025).

**Step 5 — Chart lookup:**
With a **lunar age of 30** and a **lunar conception month of 8**, the tool finds the intersection of row 30 and column 8 on the Qing Gong Biao. In the standard traditional chart, this coordinate yields the prediction **Girl**.

> **Note:** This is an illustrative example only. Your actual result depends on the precise lunisolar conversion for your specific dates. Always use the automated tool for correct calendar handling.

## What the Prediction Can — and Cannot — Tell You

The traditional Qing Gong Biao prediction is a **cultural folk tradition**, not a medical assessment. Large-scale scientific studies — including an analysis of 2.8 million birth records — have consistently found that the chart's accuracy is approximately 50%, which is statistically equivalent to chance.

Biologically, fetal sex is determined at fertilization by the chromosome carried by the fertilizing sperm: an X chromosome produces a girl (XX), and a Y chromosome produces a boy (XY). Maternal age and the calendar month have no influence over which sperm fertilizes the egg.

The prediction from this tool:
- **Is** a culturally meaningful connection to traditional Chinese folk practices.
- **Is** a lighthearted way to engage with an ancient tradition during pregnancy.
- **Is not** a substitute for clinical fetal sex determination.

For medically verified sex determination, consult your healthcare provider about cell-free DNA (NIPT) testing, available from around 10 weeks, or an anatomy ultrasound scan, typically at 18–22 weeks.

## Medical Disclaimer

The Chinese Gender Predictor by Conception Date is an ancient cultural tradition provided strictly for personal entertainment and family curiosity. It carries no clinical, scientific, or medical validity. Fetal sex is determined genetically at fertilization by whether an X-bearing or Y-bearing sperm fertilizes the egg, and is unrelated to maternal age or the calendar month.

For medically verified fetal sex determination, consult your obstetrician, midwife, or qualified healthcare provider. Clinical options include non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), mid-trimester anatomy ultrasound, chorionic villus sampling (CVS), or amniocentesis.
