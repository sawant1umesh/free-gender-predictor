The **Qing Gong Biao** (清宫表, literally "Qing Palace Table" or "Qing Imperial Chart") is the authentic historical document from which all modern Chinese Gender Predictors originate. 

For centuries, this manuscript has captivated royal courtiers, historians, and modern parents alike. But how much of its dramatic story is historical reality, and how much is imperial romance?

---

## What Is the Qing Gong Biao?

In Chinese tradition, the Qing Gong Biao is a structured matrix used to forecast whether an unborn baby will be a boy (男) or a girl (女). 

The chart pairs two specific variables:
1. **Vertical Axis (Rows):** The mother's traditional Chinese lunar age (*Xusui*, 虚岁), typically ranging from 18 to 45.
2. **Horizontal Axis (Columns):** The astronomical Chinese lunar month of conception (Months 1 through 12).

Where the row and column intersect on the grid, the chart yields its traditional prediction.

---

## The Legend of the Royal Imperial Tomb

According to enduring folklore, the Qing Gong Biao was created over **700 years ago** during the Yuan or Qing Dynasty. 

Imperial records suggest that royal court astrologers and scholars developed the chart exclusively for the Emperor and imperial concubines. In dynastic China, the birth of male heirs was of paramount political importance for succession, ensuring dynastic stability and military lineage. Consequently, the chart was classified as a state secret, accessible only within the Forbidden City.

### The Boxer Rebellion & Discovery
Legend states that during the **Boxer Rebellion (1900)**, the original parchment was seized from an imperial tomb near Beijing when foreign allied troops entered the capital. 

The original manuscript was supposedly transported to England, where it was translated and kept in royal archives. Decades later, a copy was reportedly repatriated to Asia, translated into modern Chinese, and published by the Institute of Science of Peking in 1972.

While this dramatic story remains popular worldwide, modern historians note that no verifiable contemporary imperial archives from the Qing court substantiate the royal tomb discovery. The legend is best appreciated as captivating cultural folklore that enhances the chart's ancient mystique.

---

## How the Chart Spread Globally

Throughout the 1970s and 1980s, copies of the Qing Gong Biao were published in Chinese community newspapers, almanacs, and family planning manuals across Hong Kong, Taiwan, and Singapore.

With the advent of the internet in the late 1990s and early 2000s, digital versions exploded in popularity in North America and Europe. Today, millions of expectant parents across all cultures use online calculators based on the Qing Gong Biao as a lighthearted pregnancy tradition.

## The Cultural Significance of the Chart

Beyond predictions, the Qing Gong Biao holds meaningful cultural value as a window into ancient Chinese cosmology:
* **The Lunisolar Calendar:** It reflects how deeply traditional Chinese life was organized around the moon's phases and solar agricultural terms (*Jieqi*).
* **Yin and Yang Balance:** Traditional commentators viewed the chart through the lens of Daoist balance—matching maternal age and seasonal cycles to universal forces.
* **Family Traditions:** It continues to serve as an engaging family conversation piece, bringing multiple generations together to compare historical charts.

---

## Scientific Validity vs. Cultural Charm

Epidemiological evaluations of the Qing Gong Biao—including large-scale reviews examining millions of birth records—consistently show an overall accuracy rate of **approximately 50%**.

Biologically, fetal sex is determined entirely at fertilization by whether the fertilizing sperm provides an X chromosome (female) or a Y chromosome (male). Neither maternal age nor the month of conception influences which sperm reaches the ovum first.
